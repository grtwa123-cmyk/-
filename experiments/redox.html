Galvanic Cell

Two metals, each in a solution of its own ions, joined by a wire and a salt bridge. The one that gives up electrons more readily dissolves; the other plates out. Nothing here computes the voltage from the Nernst equation — each electrode is given the rate of its forward and reverse reaction, and the voltmeter reads whatever potential makes the two run at the same speed. That turns out to be Nernst's.

Formulas
  • Cell: A(s) + Bᶻ⁺ → Aᶻ⁺ + B(s)
  • Electrode kinetics: i/i₀ = e^(u/2) − c·e^(−u/2), u = zF(E−E°)/RT
  • At i = 0: E = E° + (RT/zF)·ln c
  • Whole cell: E = E°cell − (RT/nF)·ln Q
  • Faraday: n = Q/(zF)
Notes
  • The 59.2 mV is not a constant of nature and it is not typed in anywhere. It is 2.303RT/F at 25 °C, and it comes out of the electrode kinetics — move the temperature slider and watch the slope move with it.
  • Pick the same metal on both sides and the standard potentials cancel exactly. The cell still has a voltage, made only of the difference in concentration, and it runs until the two beakers agree.
  • A heavier load pulls the voltage down. Part of that is the solution's resistance and part is the electrodes themselves needing an overpotential to run fast — the same expression that gives Nernst at rest gives Tafel's slope here.
  • Wire it backwards — the noble metal as the anode — and nothing happens. The reaction is uphill, and a cell cannot be persuaded by wiring. That is what the electrolysis page is for.
