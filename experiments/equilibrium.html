Chemical Equilibrium & Le Chatelier

A + B ⇌ C, run one molecule at a time. Every pair that meets may join; every C may come apart again; each happens with the rate constant its own energy barrier gives it. The equilibrium constant is never entered anywhere — it is measured from the counts once they settle, and what comes out is k₀/k₋. Disturb the mixture and the shift you see is a result, not a rule that was applied.

Formulas
  • Reaction: A + B ⇌ C
  • Rates: r₀ = k₀[A][B], r₋ = k₋[C]
  • At equilibrium: r₀ = r₋ → K = [C]/([A][B]) = k₀/k₋
  • Arrhenius: k = A⋅e⁻ᶜᵃ̟ᵣᶜᵗ, Eₐ(rev) = Eₐ(fwd) − ΔH°
  • van ’t Hoff: ln K = ΔS°/R − ΔH°/RT
What to look for
  • K is never entered. Each molecule reacts or comes apart at random with the rate constant its barrier gives it, and K is read off the counts afterwards. It lands on k₀/k₋ because at equilibrium the two rates are equal — which is the derivation, run forwards.
  • Equilibrium is not the reaction stopping. Watch the event counters: at equilibrium thousands of forward and reverse reactions keep happening and their ratio sits at 1.000. The concentrations hold still because the two directions cancel, not because anything has finished.
  • Now disturb it. Inject A and the mixture makes more C; squeeze the vessel and it shifts to the side with fewer molecules; heat an exothermic reaction and it retreats. In every case K itself is unchanged unless you change the temperature — what moves is Q, which then walks back to K.
  • The catalyst is the honest test of all this. Lower both barriers by the same amount and the reaction arrives at equilibrium far sooner — a hundred times sooner at the far end of the slider — while K and the final concentrations do not move at all. A catalyst changes the road, not the destination.
