Gene Expression — Where a Poisson Comes From

Every cell here is identical and none of them is told what to hold. A gene switches on and off, transcribes while it is on, and each molecule eventually degrades — that is the whole mechanism, written in molecules and coin flips. Look across the cells afterwards and the spread of their counts is not arbitrary: leave the gene on and the variance comes out equal to the mean, exactly. Let it start switching and the extra width tells you how big the bursts were.

Formulas
  • Mean copy number: m = k·kon / (γ(kon+koff))
  • Fano factor: F = 1 + k·koff / ((kon+koff)(kon+koff+γ))
  • Gene never off: F = 1
  • Fraction of time on: kon / (kon+koff)
Notes
  • The mechanism has no distribution in it. A gene flips between two states, transcribes while it is on, and each molecule degrades on its own. The shape across the cells is a consequence, and the page counts it rather than drawing it.
  • Set the gene to never switch off and the Fano factor — variance divided by mean — goes to 1 and stays there whatever the other dials say. That is the signature of a Poisson, and it is the reason counting noise is called Poisson noise in the first place.
  • Start switching the gene off and the width grows past the mean. Rare, productive bursts spread the population out much further than steady transcription at the same average, so two genes with identical mean expression can be quite different cells to be.
  • The measurement is one snapshot across the cells, not an average over time. The cells are independent of one another, which is what makes a snapshot a clean sample; successive snapshots of the same field are not independent, so nothing here averages them together.
  • The pale curve over the histogram is the Poisson with the same mean as the measurement. It is drawn for comparison and never read back into the model.
