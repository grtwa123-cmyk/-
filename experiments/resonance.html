Driven Oscillation & Resonance

Push a spring at the wrong rate and almost nothing happens. Push it at its own rate and the swing builds until damping alone holds it back — by a factor of Q, which for a lightly damped system is a large number. The mass here is integrated straight from its equation of motion; the amplitude it settles at is then measured back out of the motion and compared with the textbook curve, so the two are never assumed to agree.

Formulas
  • Equation of motion: ẍ + 2ζω₀ẋ + ω₀²x = X₀ω₀²cos ωt
  • Amplitude: A = X₀ / √((1−r²)² + (2ζr)²), r = ω/ω₀
  • Phase lag: tan φ = 2ζr / (1−r²)
  • Amplitude resonance: r = √(1−2ζ²)
  • Quality factor: Q = 1/2ζ = ω₀/Δω
What to look for
  • At resonance the amplitude is Q times what the same force produces pushed slowly. Nothing amplifies the force — each push simply arrives while the mass is already moving the right way, and the energy accumulates until damping carries it off as fast as it arrives.
  • Watch the phase, not just the size. Well below resonance the mass moves with the force; well above it moves against it; and at f₀ exactly it lags by a quarter cycle, for every damping value there is. That 90° crossing is the sharpest way to find a resonance in a real measurement.
  • The peak is not quite at f₀. Amplitude peaks at √(1−2ζ²) — slightly below — and past ζ = 1/√2 there is no peak at all, just a curve sliding downhill. Velocity and absorbed power, by contrast, always peak exactly at f₀.
  • Press Reset and watch the start. The mass does not jump to its final swing: the transient beats against the drive and dies away as e−ζω₀t, which is why a high-Q system is both the most dramatic at resonance and the slowest to get there.
