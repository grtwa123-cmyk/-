Natural Selection & Genetic Drift

One gene, two alleles, and three genotypes that survive at different rates. Give them equal odds and the allele frequencies never move — that is Hardy–Weinberg. Tilt the odds and an allele sweeps to fixation, or vanishes, or, if the heterozygote is the fittest, settles at a balanced frequency and stays there. A real population of finite size runs alongside the infinite-population curve, so you can see selection as the trend and drift as the wobble around it.

Formulas
  • Hardy–Weinberg: p² : 2pq : q²
  • Mean fitness: w̄ = p²w₉₉ + 2pq·w₉₊ + q²w₊₊
  • Next generation: p′ = (p²w₉₉ + pq·w₉₊) / w̄
  • Change: Δp = pq[p(w₉₉−w₉₊) + q(w₉₊−w₊₊)] / w̄
  • Equilibrium: p* = (w₉₊−w₊₊) / (2w₉₊−w₉₉−w₊₊)
What to look for
  • With equal fitnesses the smooth curve is a flat line — Hardy–Weinberg. The real population still wanders off it, and in a small enough population it wanders all the way to fixation. That wandering is genetic drift, and it is the only thing happening here.
  • Set aa lethal and A still never quite finishes the job: the recessive allele hides inside heterozygotes, so its frequency falls as qₔ = q₀/(1 + tq₀) — hyperbolic, not exponential. Removing the last copies takes longer than removing the first ones.
  • Make the heterozygote fittest and neither allele can win. Both are dragged to p* and held there, which is how a harmful allele stays common — the sickle-cell case. Make the heterozygote the worst instead and p* flips into a watershed: start either side of it and the population runs away from it.
  • Mean fitness never falls while the fitnesses are held constant, so the population climbs its own fitness landscape. The readout tracks it live, and the faint lines are five more populations under identical rules — same selection, different luck.
