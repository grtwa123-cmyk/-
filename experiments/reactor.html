Nuclear Reactor — From Criticality to Meltdown

A 3000 MW pressurised-water core, run from the mechanism rather than from a script. Neutrons multiply and decay with their six delayed groups, the fuel and the water push back on the reactivity as they heat, fission products keep the core warm long after the rods are in, and hot zircaloy burns in steam. Take the pumps away and none of it has to be told what to do next.

Formulas
  • Point kinetics: dn/dt = (ρ − β)/Λ · n + Σ λᵢcᵢ
  • Inhour equation: ρ = ωΛ + Σ βᵢω/(ω + λᵢ)
  • Prompt jump: n → β/(β − ρ)
  • Feedback: ρ = ρ_rod + α_F ΔT_F + α_M ΔT_C
  • Decay heat: P/P₀ = 0.066 · t^(−0.2)
  • Zircaloy oxidation: w² = A·e^(−B/RT)·t
  • Hydrogen: Zr + 2H₂O → ZrO₂ + 2H₂
Notes
  • The period on the panel is measured, not looked up: a straight line is fitted through ln n over the last half-minute of plant time and inverted. The inhour equation is solved separately, from the reactivity the core happens to have, and the two are printed side by side so you can watch them agree.
  • Pull a little reactivity in and the power jumps almost at once, then climbs slowly. The jump is the prompt neutrons, which live twenty microseconds; the climb waits on the delayed ones, which are six tenths of a percent of the total and take up to eighty seconds to appear. Those six tenths of a percent are the entire reason a reactor can be controlled by hand.
  • The core corrects itself. Hotter fuel broadens the U-238 resonances and absorbs more neutrons; hotter water is thinner and moderates worse. Both are negative, so a rod step settles at a new power where the temperature rise has cancelled it exactly — no controller involved.
  • A scram does not switch the heat off. Thirteen groups of fission products keep decaying, and their heat is integrated here rather than looked up — an hour after shutdown the core is still making about 1% of rated power, which for this plant is thirty megawatts with nowhere to go.
  • Take the heat sink away and that thirty megawatts boils the primary inventory. The level falls, the top of the core comes out of the water, and bare rods in steam are cooled about five thousand times worse than rods under boiling water. Above 1100 K the zircaloy starts burning in the steam, which releases more heat than the decay does and produces hydrogen — the runaway that makes a meltdown a meltdown rather than a slow overheat.
  • Two thermal zones, not one: the part of the core under water and the part standing in steam, each with its own fuel and cladding temperature, and a boundary that moves with the level. Within a zone it is still one temperature — there is no hot channel and no axial profile. That is enough to get the shape right. With no cooling at all this core reaches saturation at nine minutes, starts uncovering at fifty, bursts its cladding at eighty-three, melts at a hundred and six and drops the lower head at two hours; Three Mile Island did the same things in the same order, and stopped where this one does not because water came back.
