Enzyme Kinetics — Michaelis–Menten

Nothing here knows the Michaelis–Menten equation. Each enzyme molecule is its own little state machine — free, holding a substrate, releasing a product, blocked by an inhibitor — and it changes state at random with the rate constants of the mechanism. Count the products and divide by the time, and the hyperbola draws itself. Add an inhibitor and watch which of the two constants moves.

Formulas
  • Mechanism: E + S ⇌ ES → E + P
  • Rate: v = Vₘₐₓ[S] / (Kₘ + [S])
  • Constants: Vₘₐₓ = kₘₐₜ[E]ₜ, Kₘ = (k₋₁+kₘₐₜ)/k₁
  • Lineweaver–Burk: 1/v = (Kₘ/Vₘₐₓ)(1/[S]) + 1/Vₘₐₓ
  • Inhibition: α = 1 + [I]/Kⁱ
What to look for
  • No line of code computes the rate law. Each molecule sits in one of four states and jumps between them at random with the mechanism's own rate constants; the products are counted and divided by the elapsed time. The hyperbola the points land on is what that bookkeeping comes to, which is exactly how Michaelis and Menten got it.
  • At [S] = Kₘ the rate is exactly half its maximum — that is the definition of Kₘ, not a coincidence, and it is why the constant is worth measuring. Below Kₘ the rate is nearly proportional to [S]; far above it, every enzyme is already busy and adding more substrate buys nothing.
  • The three inhibitors each move exactly one thing, and the Lineweaver–Burk plot is built to show which. A competitive inhibitor raises Kₘ and leaves Vₘₐₓ alone, so the lines pivot about a shared y-intercept. A non-competitive one lowers Vₘₐₓ and leaves Kₘ, so they share the x-intercept. An uncompetitive one divides both, so the slope never changes and the lines run parallel.
  • Watch the scatter, not just the curve. Every point is a finite count of random events, so it carries real noise that shrinks as √N — and the straight-line fit recovers Kₘ and Vₘₐₓ from those noisy points, which is the whole reason the plot was invented.
  • The substrate is never used up. Every enzyme draws on the concentration on the slider as if from an endless pool, which is the initial-rate assay an experimentalist sets up on purpose — measure fast, before [S] has fallen. It is also why Michaelis–Menten is exact here rather than approximate: the law assumes a substrate the enzymes cannot deplete, and this one genuinely cannot be. Run it long enough and the products counted will pass the whole pool several times over.
