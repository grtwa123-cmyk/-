The Coalescent — Where a Family Tree Comes From

A population of a fixed size, and one rule: every individual picks a parent at random from the generation before. Nothing is being selected for and nobody has more children on purpose. Now take a handful of them alive today and follow their parents backwards. Sooner or later two of the lines land on the same individual — not because anything drew them together, but because there are only so many parents to pick from — and where there were two lines there is now one. Keep going and the whole sample closes down to a single ancestor. Every tree is different; the theory is a statement about their average, which is why this page keeps making new ones.

Formulas
  • The rule: each individual picks a parent uniformly at random
  • Time with k lineages: E[T_k] = 2N / k(k−1)
  • Depth of the tree: E[T_MRCA] = 2N(1 − 1/n)
  • Total branch length: E[L] = 2N · H(n−1)
  • Watterson: θ̂ = S / H(n−1) estimates 2Nμ
  • Exactly, in a finite population: q_k = ∏(N−i)/N, E[T_k] = 1/(1 − q_k)
Notes
  • Nothing here is drawn towards anything. Two lineages merge only because both drew the same number out of N, and the whole shape of a genealogy — how deep it is, how long its branches are, how much variation it can carry — comes out of that one collision probability. There is no selection anywhere in this page.
  • Most of a family tree is two lineages waiting. The last pair takes 2N/2 = N generations to find each other, which is over half of the tree's whole depth of 2N(1 − 1/n) no matter how big the sample is. Take a thousand people instead of ten and the tree barely gets deeper — the extra lineages merge almost at once and then everyone waits.
  • Watch the histogram, not one tree. The depth of a single tree is nearly useless: its spread is about six tenths of its own mean, so a tree twice as deep as average is unremarkable. That is not measurement noise, it is the thing itself — and it is why a formula about the average needs hundreds of trees to be seen at all.
  • Mutations fall on the branches at a fixed rate, so counting them measures the total branch length, and dividing by H(n−1) turns that into θ = 2Nμ. That is Watterson's estimator, used on real sequence data to ask how big a population was, and here you can watch it come back out of a cartoon.
  • The textbook formulas are a limit, not an identity. They hold when the number of lineages squared is small next to the population; here it need not be. With ten lineages in a population of sixteen, 2N/(k(k−1)) says the ten will last 0.36 generations and the truth is 1.03, because ten lineages in sixteen slots cannot all avoid each other for even one generation. The panel shows both, and the gap between them is the size of the approximation.
