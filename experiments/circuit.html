Ohm's Law — Series & Parallel

Three resistors, one cell, and a choice of how to wire them. In series there is a single path, so the same current is forced through every resistor and the voltages add up. In parallel each resistor bridges the same two nodes, so each gets the full cell voltage and the currents add up instead. The charge carriers move at a speed set by the real current in each wire, so you can see the rail slow down as every branch takes its share.

Formulas
  • Ohm's law: V = I R
  • Series: R = R₁+R₂+R₃, I shared
  • Parallel: 1/R = 1/R₁+1/R₂+1/R₃, V shared
  • Power: P = V I = I²R
What to look for
  • In series there is only one path, so every resistor carries the same current and the voltage divides between them in proportion to resistance. Adding a resistor always raises the total.
  • In parallel every resistor sees the full cell voltage and draws its own current. Adding a resistor always lowers the total — the combined resistance is smaller than the smallest branch on its own.
  • Watch the top rail in parallel: each segment carries only the current that has not yet been tapped off, so the carriers visibly slow down from left to right. The bodies warm with the power they dissipate, on an absolute scale.
  • Both arrangements are the same two Kirchhoff rules seen from different sides — voltages around a loop sum to zero, currents into a node sum to zero. Both residuals are printed under the circuit and stay at zero as you drag.
